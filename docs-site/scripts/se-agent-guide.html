Part one

What is this thing, really?

A helper that works inside the code, not a chatbot that talks about it.

Imagine hiring someone on their first day. They don't know where anything is. But they are quick, they never get bored, and they have three good habits:

They look before they touch. They don't guess where the login code lives; they go and find it. The agent is not even allowed to change a file it hasn't read, and if the file changed since it looked, the change is refused.

They ask before anything risky. Running commands, changing settings, opening a file that looks like it holds a password: they stop and check with you first, showing exactly what they are about to do.

They show their receipts. When they're done, they list every file they touched, with a side-by-side comparison and a single button that puts everything back.

That is the whole product. Everything technical exists to force those three habits onto a computer program that would otherwise happily guess.

The one-sentence version
A helper that reads and changes our code on its own, shows every step, asks permission before anything it can't undo, and can run entirely on our own computers so client code never leaves the building.

What it is not

It is not a chatbot that answers questions. A chatbot talks; this one acts: it opens files, edits them, runs the tests, reads the errors and tries again. And it is not a replacement for a developer. It is very good at the boring, repetitive half of the job and genuinely unreliable at deciding what should be built.

Part two

How it works, step by step

One instruction from you turns into a loop of small steps. The loop is the whole trick.

Steps two to five repeat until the work is actually done. That is what makes it an "agent" rather than something that gives you an answer and stops.

While it works you see every step as a line in the chat: "Read src/auth.ts", "Search login", "Run npm test". You can keep typing to steer it ("actually, put it in the middleware"), and the Esc key stops it at any time.

Why this matters for cost and speed
Every one of those steps is a separate question to the AI. A simple edit is three to eight steps; a big change can be twenty to sixty. So when a free plan "allows 1,000 requests a day", that is not 1,000 tasks; it is more like 30 to 100. This is the single most misunderstood number in the whole subject.

Helpers for big searches

For a big search, the agent sends a junior helper who reads a hundred files in its own room and comes back with a six-line answer. The hundred files never land on the main desk (see Part three). Up to four helpers can work at once, which is why it copes with a large codebase on a small model.

Part three

Three words you'll keep hearing

Model, tokens, context window. Everything else is detail.

The model is the brain

The agent itself has no intelligence. It is the hands, the eyes and the rulebook. The thinking is done by a separate thing called a model, and we can plug in different brains: one running on your own laptop, one on a company server, or one rented over the internet. Same agent, different brain, very different speed and cost.

Tokens are pieces of words

Models read tokens: chunks of about four characters. A rough rule: 750 words is about 1,000 tokens. You pay for tokens, and the brain can only hold so many at once.

The context window is the desk

Picture a desk that fits a fixed number of pages. Everything the assistant is thinking about has to be on it: the house rules, your request, every file it has read, every test result. When the desk fills up, something has to go.

It clears space in a fixed order: first old tool output becomes a one-line note, then, only if needed, it asks the AI to write a summary. The house rules never move, because keeping them identical is what lets the server answer faster on every step after the first.

In normal numbers
Our default desk is 32,768 tokens: roughly 24,000 words, or about 50 pages. A big model on a good server can have a desk four times that size. But bigger is not automatically better: models get noticeably worse at finding things in the middle of a very full desk, which is why we tidy it even when there is room.
Part four

What's inside our agent

Four layers, each with one job. Nothing skips a layer, which is why it's safe.

The chat panel is deliberately powerless: it can only show things and pass on what you type. Anything that touches a real file goes through the safety guard first.

Three ways to teach it

A rules file. Short, always-true facts ("run tests with npm test", "we use tabs") go in a file called AGENTS.md that it reads every time.

Skills. A folder where we write down bigger house rules: "always use our DataGrid, never a plain table". The assistant only reads them when the job actually involves that topic, so they cost almost nothing the rest of the time. Like a recipe book on the shelf rather than read aloud at every meal.

Plugs (MCP servers). Small programs that give it new abilities, such as asking our component library what a component's real options are. Part eight explains them.

Part five

Where the brain lives, and what that costs

Three options. They trade privacy, speed and money against each other.

1. On your laptop

Like cooking at home.

+ Free, works on a plane, nothing ever leaves.

− Slower, and the brain must fit your machine.

2. On a company server

Like a staff canteen.

+ A big brain, fast, shared, code stays with us.

− Costs money up front and needs setup.

3. Rented online

Like a takeaway.

+ Strongest brains, no hardware, free tiers to try.

− Our code goes to someone else's computer.

The agent works with all three, and you switch with two clicks. Option 1 is the one nobody else makes easy, and our distinguishing feature. Before anything is sent to an outside service the first time, the agent asks, and a "cloud" badge stays on screen from then on.

Why the rented one sometimes says "server overload"

When you rent, you share. A free plan is a busy shop at lunchtime: sometimes the answer is "too many people right now, come back in a minute". That is the "429" error. It is a queue, not a bug. The agent waits a second and tries again, then two seconds, then four, and tells you it is waiting instead of looking frozen.

If we run our own server

This is buying the pizza oven instead of ordering delivery. It makes sense at volume, and the sums are about memory on the graphics card, not speed. Each conversation the brain is holding takes memory, and a bigger desk takes proportionally more: a mid-size brain with a very large desk can use 30–40 GB of card memory per person using it at that moment.

The one free win
Our agent sends the same house rules at the start of every step. Server software can be told to remember that repeated opening (it's called prefix caching), and it makes every step after the first much faster for the price of one setting. Turn it on from day one.
Part six

How it keeps you in control

The safety guard, the receipts, and the dials.

The safety guard

There are three settings, chosen with one click: Ask first (it asks about everything), Auto-edit (the default: ordinary edits go ahead, commands ask) and Full auto (it never asks; for trusted work only). Whatever the setting, outside Full auto it always asks before it changes files that control tools, scripts or settings, opens a file that looks like it holds a password, or runs a command that chains several programs together.

"Always allow" is a narrow yes: it remembers "npm test" for the rest of the session, not "run anything".

The receipts

Every task ends with a list of changed files, a side-by-side comparison for each one, and an Undo button that puts every file back the way it was before the task started. It warns you first if you have edited one of those files yourself since.

The dials

Safety caps stop a runaway task burning money, one giant file flooding the desk, or an endless loop. They were set for small brains; with a better brain, some should go up.

The honest gap: the brain

Most of what makes Claude Code and Codex feel so capable is the brain behind them, trained specifically for this kind of work. On a small local model our agent is less capable on hard tasks, however good the rest of it is. That is why moving to our own, larger self-hosted model matters more than any feature.

The fair summary
For a company that cannot send client code to the cloud, ours is a genuinely good answer and the others are not. For a developer who just wants the strongest results and doesn't mind where the code goes, the others are still ahead on hard tasks. A stronger self-hosted model is what closes that gap.
Part eight

The universal plug is built

MCP is a standard socket. Build it once, and the assistant can talk to anything that has the matching plug.

Before, the assistant could do exactly ten things, all about files and commands. Reading a design from Figma would have meant Figma-specific code, then Jira-specific code, then database-specific code, forever. MCP is the agreed shape of the plug, the USB of AI tools. We have now built the socket, and every future connection is a line of configuration instead of a development project.

What it gives us today

Our own component library, GramproKit, ships a plug. With it, the assistant asks the library what a component's real options are instead of guessing, installs the pieces it needs, and then has its own code checked against the real definitions. Combined with our house rules (skills), you get code that matches both the design and our standards, something none of the big names offer a Grampro client out of the box.

Why it is safe

A plug defined by a project we open is, in effect, permission to run a program on our machine. So the agent asks once, clearly, showing the exact command, and asks again if that command ever changes. Each time a plug is used, it asks again unless you said "always allow" for that one action.
